# Parameter estimation of a whole-brain model with a hybrid physics-informed neural network

## 1. Objective and scope

We ask what the resting-state BOLD of one subject determines about a whole-brain Montbrió–Pazó–Roxin (MPR) model. The aim is to estimate the global coupling $G$ and the regional excitabilities $\eta_i$ of an MPR whole-brain model from one subject's real resting-state fMRI, to decide the dynamical regime, bistable with regions switching between two states or monostable with noise around one equilibrium, from the data instead of assuming it, to measure the reliability of the estimates, and to reconstruct the regional states with a noise-aware PINN. The claim is one of inference, which is stronger than a good fit: a parameter set or a regime that reproduces the data is not enough if the data do not determine it.

**Central problem.** Regime-assumed estimates of regional excitabilities $\eta_i$, of unknown reliability, in whole-brain MPR models fitted to single-subject resting-state fMRI.

**Direct causes.**

- **DC1.** Static FC fits do not discriminate regional switching between states from noise around one equilibrium, and whether resting-state BOLD requires nonlinear, let alone multistable, dynamics is contested. Linear auto-regressive models describe the resting-state fMRI of 700 HCP subjects best (Nozari et al. 2024), whereas a close relative of the MPR model, a network of quadratic integrate-and-fire neurons with Cauchy-distributed excitability and chemical and electrical synapses, reproduces the data better in its nonlinear regime (Forrester et al. 2024). The debate is between linear and nonlinear dynamics, not between bistable and monostable ones.
- **DC2.** Functional features alone (FC, FCD) carry insufficient information to determine regional excitabilities, so fits improve while regional estimates vary across optimization runs. In simulation, FC and FCD alone are insufficient for a heterogeneous excitability (Hashemi et al. 2024), and without spatio-temporal features the $\eta_i$ are poorly estimated while $G$ is recovered from FC and FCD alone (Ziaeemehr et al. 2025). With phase oscillators fitted to 272 HCP subjects, regional parameters improve the fit but vary across 30 repeated optimization runs (Wischnewski et al. 2025).
- **DC3.** The regime is fixed by assumption before fitting, and noise, when estimated, is estimated conditional on it, without testing how the regime verdict depends on either. Rabuffo et al. (2021), Hashemi et al. (2024) and Ziaeemehr et al. (2025) tune the decoupled node bistable, and Lavanga et al. (2023), with the MPR model on 649 subjects of 1000BRAINS, fix $\eta=-4.6$ and fit $G$ and the noise variance per subject.

**Effects.**

- **EF1.** Mechanistic readings of fitted MPR models, such as neuronal cascades, rest on a dynamical regime the data have not been shown to support.
- **EF2.** Regional parameters of whole-brain models are read as subject-level markers, yet for the MPR model their test-retest reliability is unknown. An excitation-inhibition ratio marker from a circuit model fitted to resting-state fMRI (Zhang et al. 2024) and regional inhibition inferred with a dynamic mean-field model in 414 participants of a 2024 dementia study in Alzheimer's Research & Therapy are such readings, neither with the MPR model. With other models, regional parameters are poor to fair in reliability across repeated optimization runs (Wischnewski et al. 2025) and across two sessions of 217 HCP subjects with a node-heterogeneous reduced Wong–Wang model (Saberi et al. 2025, cuBNM). For the MPR model, no reviewed source reports test-retest reliability; this is to be verified by a bounded search (Section 7).

Fits of whole-brain models to real data of individual subjects exist and do not remove this gap: Lavanga et al. (2023), above; Wischnewski et al. (2025); Domhof et al. (2022); and Saberi et al. (2025, cuBNM). To our knowledge, no study has compared a bistable and a monostable configuration of the same biophysical neural mass model on real resting-state fMRI; a bounded search of the literature on 2026-11-09 found none (Section 7).

The pipeline from connectome to neural masses to BOLD to FC is established (Honey et al. 2009; Cabral et al. 2011; Deco and Jirsa 2012; Sanz-Leon et al. 2013), and so is the whole-brain MPR model fitted to FC with a homogeneous excitability (Rabuffo et al. 2021). Its parameters are estimated by parameter sweeps, CMA-ES and Bayesian optimisation (Wischnewski et al. 2022), CMA-ES with regional maps (Kong et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2024), GPU CMA-ES on individual subjects with test-retest (Saberi et al. 2025), simulation-based inference (Ziaeemehr et al. 2025) and gradients through a differentiable simulator (TVB-Optim 2025). A PINN has estimated the parameters of a phenomenological brain dynamics model, including directed connectivity, from resting-state fMRI (Sotero et al. 2024), but no PINN has been applied to the whole-brain MPR model with real BOLD. Among the estimators used with whole-brain MPR models (parameter sweeps, CMA-ES and simulation-based inference), the PINN is the one that returns the hidden neural trajectories together with the parameters; stochastic DCM and Kalman-type filters estimate hidden states in other models (Li et al. 2011; Razi et al. 2015). In this proposal the PINN is one estimator among those compared, and its specific role is to reconstruct which state each region occupies over time, which is tested on synthetic data (H4). Only states that last long enough for the BOLD to resolve them, of the order of seconds, are recoverable.

The work proceeds in four stages, and the last three are read only after the first holds. First, on synthetic BOLD generated in each configuration, it is established which statistics identify the regime and recover the parameters, whether the estimation target is the free $\eta_i$ or the map of Eq. (15), and at what number of volumes (H1); this stage also decides which dataset the later stages use. Second, the bistable and the monostable configuration are fitted to the same real data by forward stochastic simulation, with a parameter sweep and CMA-ES, and read against a linear floor (H2). Third, the reliability of the estimates is measured across seeds, initial values and sessions (H3). Fourth, the noise-aware hybrid PINN is compared with the forward benchmark and tested as a reconstructor of regional states (H4).

Scope: one subject of a resting-state dataset, parcellated into $N$ regions, with $T$ volumes at repetition time TR. The dataset is not fixed and is not tied to 1000BRAINS: H1 states the number of volumes the comparison of regimes needs, and the dataset is chosen accordingly. The method is written for any such instance; the instance of Section 3.1 is the provisional one inherited from revision r12, and every value that depends on it is given there, so changing the dataset or the subject changes only that subsection. Every parameter that is not estimated is fixed and declared. Extending the regional parametrisation to a cohort, to ask how $\eta_0$ and $a$ change with age, and using the estimated parameters to explore perturbations that could inform the treatment of disorders are future work and motivation, not results of this proposal.

The formulations that revision r13 listed as owed are given in this revision: the forcing term through which the PINN represents the noise (Eqs. (24) and (25), Section 5); the linear floor of H2 (Eq. (26), Section 3); the noise level, fixed and read along a grid (Section 3); the bistable and the monostable configuration, defined by the convention of the literature (Section 3); and the statistics of H1 and H2 (Section 6). Equations (1) to (23) keep their meaning; the forcing term is added to Eqs. (16), (18) and (19).

Decisions that depend on forward simulations (the value of $G_0$ and the useful range of $G$, the scale $\chi$ of the Balloon input, heterogeneous $\eta_i$ in the generator, the scale of the physics residual of Eq. (2)) are not fixed here. They belong to the simulation stages of the project and are listed in Section 7. The noise level is not among them: the grid of $\sigma_\xi$ of Section 3 covers it.

## 2. Hypotheses

These hypotheses supersede those of revision r13.

- **H1 (identifiability on synthetic data; required gate).** On synthetic BOLD of both configurations, preprocessed as the real data (detrending, GSR), FC, FCD and scale-free regional statistics identify the regime and recover $G$ and free $\eta_i$, or else the map $\eta_0+a\,m_i$, within the pilot tolerance, across the $\sigma_\xi$ grid. H1 decides the estimation target, the free $\eta_i$ when they are recovered with statistics that survive the preprocessing of the chosen data and otherwise the map, reported as a result, and the number of volumes, hence the dataset. Results on real data are interpreted only after H1 holds.
- **H2 (regime on real data).** On real single-subject BOLD, bistable and monostable fits differ in held-out dynamic statistics by more than seed and, when available, session variability, read against a linear floor and reported along the $\sigma_\xi$ grid. Both outcomes are reported: whether resting-state BOLD favours regional switching between two states is contested (Rabuffo et al. 2021; Kong et al. 2021; Sip et al. 2023; Nozari et al. 2024).
- **H3 (reliability).** Repeated fits with different seeds, initial values and, when available, a second session give the same $G$ and $\eta_i$ within their spread, with the mapped parametrisation $\eta_i=\eta_0+a\,m_i$ as comparison. When no second session exists, the two halves of the session take its place.
- **H4 (PINN).** A noise-aware PINN recovers on synthetic data the fraction of time each region spends in each state, and on real data its estimates give an FC no worse than sweep and CMA-ES under a declared simulation budget. Success is read against the ceilings of Section 6, not against $\rho(\mathrm{SC},\mathrm{FC})$ (0.28 in the instance of Section 3.1), which is only a lower reference, and H4 can fail without affecting H1 to H3.

## 3. Model

Each region $i$ carries one MPR neural mass, coupled through the structural connectome $A$ (fibre counts without diagonal, normalised to a maximum of 1), without delays in version 1:

$$
\tau_m \dot r_i = \frac{\Delta}{\pi \tau_m} + 2 r_i V_i \tag{1}
$$

$$
\tau_m \dot V_i = V_i^2 + \eta_i - (\pi \tau_m r_i)^2 + \tau_m J r_i + \tau_m G \sum_j A_{ij} r_j \tag{2}
$$

with fixed $\Delta=0.7$ and $J=14.5$, taken from Rabuffo et al. (2021), who fit them so that an isolated region is bistable. The time constant is $\tau_m=1$ ms. Rabuffo et al. use a dimensionless characteristic time $\tau_c=1$ and an integration step of 1 ms ("2000 steps are 2 s"), so one unit of model time corresponds to 1 ms, and Hashemi et al. (2024) work with the same model and the same convention of regions switching between states. The value $\tau_m=1$ ms is therefore a declared assumption supported by that coherence, not a published measurement. A value of 10 ms is used as a sensitivity test. The initial value of $\eta_i$ is $-4.6$, the homogeneous value of Rabuffo et al., inside the bistable range $(-5.35,-2.45)$ that was computed here for $\Delta=0.7$ and $J=14.5$ and is not reported in that paper. $G_0$ is the inverse of the mean row sum of $A$, a unit of scale and not a value taken from the literature:

$$
G_0 = \frac{N}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N} A_{ij}} \tag{3}
$$

**Noise.** Forward simulations used as data generators and for the checks of Section 6 add a Gaussian noise $\xi_i$ of zero mean to the right side of Eq. (2), in the same position as Hashemi et al. (2024, Section 2.2, Eq. 1 and Figure S1). The PINN represents the slow part of this noise with the forcing term $u_i$ of Eq. (24) (Section 5):

$$
\tau_m \dot V_i = V_i^2 + \eta_i - (\pi \tau_m r_i)^2 + \tau_m J r_i + \tau_m G \sum_j A_{ij} r_j + \xi_i(t), \qquad \xi_i(t)\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_\xi^2) \tag{4}
$$

Hashemi et al. state $\xi(t)\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$ with variance $\sigma^2=0.03$, written $\sigma_\xi^2$ here because $\sigma$ with a subscript denotes the standard deviation of the quantity it names, and report that, with this noise, the regions are in the bistable regime around $G=0.5$. The noise level is fixed at their value, $\sigma_\xi^2=0.03$, and is not estimated, because they use the same MPR model and the convention $\tau_m=1$ ms adopted here. The value $0.037$ of Ziaeemehr et al. (2025, Table 5) is not copied, because their noise convention, integrator and step are not verified. The same value is used wherever the noise level enters, the penalty of Eq. (25) included, so the simulated and trained quantities are comparable. The regime verdict of H1 and H2 is reported along a grid of $\sigma_\xi$ that covers the variance and the standard-deviation readings of $0.03$, as Ziaeemehr et al. (2025, Figure 7, supplements 6 and 7) evaluate estimation across levels of additive and dynamical noise; they also note that estimating the noise "can also be challenging". Three things are verified and three are not. Verified: the time step of the source model is 1 ms with $\tau_c=1$ (Rabuffo et al. 2021), the convention adopted here for $\tau_m$; the documentation of The Virtual Brain, the software of both papers, defines its additive noise parameter `nsig` as a standard deviation, not a variance; and its stochastic examples for this model use a stochastic Heun integrator. Not verified: whether Hashemi et al. passed $0.03$ to the software as a variance or as `nsig`, which differ by a factor of about 5.8 in amplitude ($\sqrt{0.03}\approx0.17$ against $0.03$ read as a standard deviation); the integrator and step they used; and how the integrator scales the noise with the step. Hashemi et al. also couple the regions diffusively, $G\sum_j SC_{ij}(r_j-r_i)$, whereas Eq. (2) couples through the additive current $G\sum_j A_{ij} r_j$; this difference is declared and not resolved.

The firing rate drives a Balloon–Windkessel model (Friston et al. 2000) through the input

$$
z_i = \chi\,(r_i - \bar r_i) \tag{5}
$$

where $\bar r_i$ is the time mean of $r_i$. Each region carries four hemodynamic states, each normalised to its value at rest: the vasodilatory signal $s_i$, the blood inflow $f_i$, the venous blood volume $\nu_i$ and the deoxyhaemoglobin content $q_i$ (Friston et al. 2000; Stephan et al. 2007, Eqs. 8 and 9; Zeidman et al. 2019, Eqs. 20 to 22):

$$
\dot s_i = z_i - \kappa s_i - \gamma (f_i - 1) \tag{6}
$$

$$
\dot f_i = s_i \tag{7}
$$

$$
\tau_h \dot \nu_i = f_i - \nu_i^{1/\alpha} \tag{8}
$$

$$
\tau_h \dot q_i = f_i \frac{E(f_i)}{E_0} - \nu_i^{1/\alpha} \frac{q_i}{\nu_i} \tag{9}
$$

where $E(f)=1-(1-E_0)^{1/f}$ is the fraction of oxygen extracted from the inflowing blood, so that $E(1)=E_0$ is its value at rest. The symbol $s_i$ denotes this state only. Eqs. (8) and (9) contain $\nu_i^{1/\alpha}$, $q_i/\nu_i$ and $(1-E_0)^{1/f_i}$, which are defined only for positive $f_i$ and $\nu_i$, and while the network is far from a solution its output can drive $f_i$ below zero. The integration therefore advances $\ln f_i$, $\ln \nu_i$ and $\ln q_i$, with $\mathrm{d}\ln x/\mathrm{d}t=\dot x/x$, as the reference implementation SPM12 does (Zeidman et al. 2019, Appendix); this change of variables keeps the three states positive while the solution of Eqs. (6) to (9) stays positive, which bounds the size of the input $z_i$; the scale $\chi$ fixed in the simulation stage must keep it inside that domain, and the change leaves the model of Eqs. (6) to (9) unchanged.

Its parameters are those of the reference implementation of dynamic causal modelling, SPM12 (`spm_fx_fmri.m`), which coincide with Zeidman et al. (2019, Table 3) where that table lists them: $\kappa=0.64$ s$^{-1}$, $\gamma=0.32$ s$^{-1}$, $\tau_h=2.00$ s, $\alpha=0.32$ and $E_0=0.4$. The value of $\gamma$ is taken from the code, because the table of Zeidman et al. does not list it. These are the defaults of an estimation tool, not measurements. The values of Friston et al. (2003, Table 1), $\kappa=0.65$ s$^{-1}$, $\gamma=0.41$ s$^{-1}$, $\tau_h=0.98$ s, $\alpha=0.32$ and $E_0=0.34$, which Heinzle et al. (2016) also use, are the sensitivity case. None of the sources read here gives the reason for the change from the second set to the first, so it is reported as undocumented. The effect of $\kappa$, $\gamma$, $\tau_h$ and $E_0$ is covered by a sensitivity analysis. The scale $\chi$ depends on the scale of $r$, which changes with $\tau_m$, and is set in the simulation stage.

**Initial state.** The integration starts at $t=0$, the time of the first volume, at rest: $s_i(0)=0$ and $f_i(0)=\nu_i(0)=q_i(0)=1$. Rest is the fixed point of Eqs. (6) to (9) for $z_i=0$, and $z_i$ has zero mean by construction, so rest is the expected state when the activity before the first volume is not observed. No warm-up interval is simulated. The state of the real subject at $t=0$ depends on that unobserved activity, so the first volumes of the simulated BOLD carry a transient of the initial state. Linearised around rest with the parameters above, Eqs. (6) and (7) have the characteristic polynomial $\lambda^2+\kappa\lambda+\gamma=0$, with roots $\lambda\approx-0.32\pm0.47i$ s$^{-1}$; their slowest mode decays at the rate $\kappa/2=0.32$ s$^{-1}$, Eqs. (8) and (9) relax faster, and the transient falls below $1\%$ of its initial size within $15$ s. With the parameters of Friston et al. (2003) the rate is about $0.33$ s$^{-1}$. These figures are computed here and hold near rest only. The sensitivity of $\rho_{\mathrm{FC}}$ to the transient is tested by discarding the first $15$ s of both the simulated and the measured series.

The BOLD signal is

$$
y_i = b_0\left[k_1(1-q_i) + k_2\left(1-\frac{q_i}{\nu_i}\right) + k_3(1-\nu_i)\right] \tag{10}
$$

with the coefficients of Stephan et al. (2007, Eq. B9) and Heinzle et al. (2016, Eq. 7),

$$
k_1 = 4.3\,\vartheta_0 E_0\,\mathrm{TE}, \qquad k_2 = \varepsilon\,\varrho_0 E_0\,\mathrm{TE}, \qquad k_3 = 1-\varepsilon, \qquad \varepsilon = e^{-(R^*_{2,I}-R^*_{2,E})\,\mathrm{TE}} \tag{11}
$$

The coefficients depend on the field strength $B_0$ and the echo time TE of the instance. Heinzle et al. (2016, Appendix A) give $\vartheta_0\approx 28.265\,B_0$ s$^{-1}$; $\varrho_0$, which Stephan et al. (2007) and Heinzle et al. (2016) write $r_0$ and which is renamed here because $r_i$ is the firing rate, is $\approx 15$ s$^{-1}$ at 1.5 T, $110$ s$^{-1}$ at 3 T and about $340$ s$^{-1}$ at 7 T; and $\varepsilon$ of Eq. (11), the ratio of intravascular to extravascular signal, with the relaxation rates $R^*_{2,I}$ and $R^*_{2,E}$ of their Table A1 for 1.5, 3 and 7 T. None of these depends on $E_0$. Heinzle et al. fix $E_0=0.34$ themselves, so the pairing of $E_0$ from SPM12 with their field coefficients is a combination made in this work. The ratio $\varepsilon$ is the least certain input: Obata et al. fixed 1.43 at 1.5 T, Buxton et al. assumed 0.4, and Stephan et al. (2007) propose to treat it as a free parameter with prior mean 1. Here $\varepsilon$ is fixed at the value given by the field and the TE of the instance. An instance acquired at a field strength that Heinzle et al. do not cover needs $\varrho_0$ and the relaxation rates from another source. The numerical values for the instance of version 1 are given in Section 3.1. The resting venous blood volume fraction is $b_0=0.02$ (Friston et al. 2003), which Friston et al. (2003) and Stephan et al. (2007) write $V_0$ and which is renamed here because $V_i$ is the membrane potential; it only scales the signal and does not affect a correlation. The sensitivity of the FC to the $k_i$ is tested.

**Regime configurations.** The two configurations follow the convention of the literature. In the bistable configuration each decoupled node has its $\eta_i$ in the bistable range $(-5.35,-2.45)$ computed here for $\Delta=0.7$ and $J=14.5$, where it has two stable fixed points, a down node and an up focus (Montbrió et al. 2015; Rabuffo et al. 2021); in the monostable configuration the $\eta_i$ lie outside that range. Whether the network switches between states is judged behaviourally, by the variance of the FCD (Hashemi et al. 2024). The coupling shifts the effective excitability of each region, so the label of the decoupled node does not guarantee the label of the network; this limit is declared (Section 7).

**Linear floor.** The lower reference of H2 is a linear network on the same connectome, with the same $G$, the same noise, the Balloon–Windkessel model of Eqs. (6) to (9) and the BOLD signal of Eq. (10), in which $x_i$ replaces the firing rate $r_i$ as the input of Eq. (5):

$$
\tau_m \dot x_i = -x_i + G\sum_j A_{ij} x_j + \xi_i(t) \tag{26}
$$

This form is defined here as the linear counterpart of Eq. (2) with one stable equilibrium, which it has when $G$ is smaller than the inverse of the largest eigenvalue of $A$. Domhof et al. (2022) compare linear, phase-oscillator and neural-mass network models, without the claim that their linear model has this form. The linear auto-regressive model of Nozari et al. (2024), fitted directly to the BOLD, is reported as a reference outside the verdict of H2, because it has more parameters and no connectome.

### 3.1 Data and instance of version 1

The method takes a structural connectome $A$ and the regional BOLD series of one subject, parcellated into $N$ regions, with $T$ volumes at repetition time TR, acquired at field strength $B_0$ with echo time TE. The simulated BOLD is sampled at the same TR and receives exactly the same preprocessing as the measured series before the FC is computed; in particular, global signal regression is applied to the simulated BOLD if and only if the instance applied it to the measured series. Let $y_i[n]$, $n=1,\dots,T$, be the BOLD of region $i$ at $t=(n-1)\,\mathrm{TR}$ after that preprocessing, measured or simulated with Eq. (10). The FC of the pair $(i,j)$ is the Pearson correlation of the two series,

$$
\mathrm{FC}_{ij} = \frac{\sum_{n=1}^{T}(y_i[n]-\bar y_i)(y_j[n]-\bar y_j)}{\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{T}(y_i[n]-\bar y_i)^2}\,\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{T}(y_j[n]-\bar y_j)^2}} \tag{12}
$$

where $\bar y_i$ is the time mean of $y_i[n]$. The simulated and measured FC are compared directly over the $P=N(N-1)/2$ pairs $i<j$ of the upper triangle, by the Pearson correlation of their entries,

$$
\rho_{\mathrm{FC}} = \frac{\sum_{i<j}\left(\mathrm{FC}^{\mathrm{sim}}_{ij}-\overline{\mathrm{FC}}{}^{\mathrm{sim}}\right)\left(\mathrm{FC}^{\mathrm{real}}_{ij}-\overline{\mathrm{FC}}{}^{\mathrm{real}}\right)}{\sqrt{\sum_{i<j}\left(\mathrm{FC}^{\mathrm{sim}}_{ij}-\overline{\mathrm{FC}}{}^{\mathrm{sim}}\right)^2}\,\sqrt{\sum_{i<j}\left(\mathrm{FC}^{\mathrm{real}}_{ij}-\overline{\mathrm{FC}}{}^{\mathrm{real}}\right)^2}} \tag{13}
$$

where $\overline{\mathrm{FC}}$ is the mean of the entries over the $P$ pairs. The Fisher $z$ transformation is not applied; it is tested as a sensitivity analysis.

**Instance of version 1.** One subject of 1000BRAINS v1.1 (Jung et al. 2023): Desikan–Killiany parcellation with $N=70$ regions, hence $P=2415$ pairs, and an EPI acquisition of 11 minutes with TR $=2.2$ s, hence $T=300$ volumes, on a 3 T Siemens Tim-TRIO with TE $=30$ ms (Caspers et al. 2014). The dataset FC is the Pearson correlation between the regional BOLD series after linear detrending and a nuisance regression that includes the global signal, so global signal regression is applied to the simulated BOLD as well. For 3 T and TE $=30$ ms, Heinzle et al. give $\vartheta_0\approx 84.8$ s$^{-1}$, $\varrho_0\approx 110$ s$^{-1}$ and $\varepsilon\approx 0.47$, which with $E_0=0.4$ yield $k_1\approx 4.38$, $k_2\approx 0.62$ and $k_3=0.53$; with $E_0=0.34$ the same inputs would give $3.72$, $0.53$ and $0.53$. These three numbers are computed here from published values; they are not published. 88 of the 261 subjects of the dataset have a second session; whether this subject is one of them has not been confirmed, and Section 6 states how the criteria read in each case. The map $m_i$ of Eq. (15) is the group-average cortical T1w/T2w ratio, a proxy of intracortical myelin (Glasser and Van Essen 2011), from the Human Connectome Project, averaged within each Desikan–Killiany region and standardised over the regions. It is a group map, independent of the subject's FC and of $A$, as in Kong et al. (2021) and Zhang et al. (2024). Regions the map does not cover take $m_i=0$, the mean of the map; which of the $N=70$ regions these are is checked when the map is built. This choice is provisional (operator decision, 2026-11-09); the strength of $A$ is excluded because it enters the coupling term of Eq. (2), and a subject-level FC gradient is excluded because it is derived from the data being fitted.

## 4. Method

A single fully connected network with three hidden layers of 100 tanh units, Glorot initialisation, maps the scaled time to the dimensionless rate and the potential of every region,

$$
(\hat r_1^*,\dots,\hat r_N^*,\hat V_1,\dots,\hat V_N) = \mathrm{NN}_\varphi(\tilde t), \qquad \tilde t = \frac{2t}{(T-1)\,\mathrm{TR}} - 1, \qquad t\in\left[0,(T-1)\,\mathrm{TR}\right] \tag{14}
$$

where $\varphi$ are the network weights, the time domain runs from the first to the last volume, $\hat r_i^*=\tau_m\hat r_i$ is the dimensionless rate of Section 5, and a softplus on the outputs $\hat r_i^*$ keeps the rate positive. The depth and width follow the PINNs of Raissi et al. (2019, 4–5 layers of 50–200 units) and the PINN of resting-state dynamics of 2024 (three layers of 100 tanh units, Glorot); the output, with $2N$ variables, is the only difference. When $2N$ exceeds the width $W=100$ of the last hidden layer, as in the instance of version 1 ($2N=140$), the $2N$ trajectories are linear combinations of $W$ functions, which limits the patterns the network can represent. The effect of a wider layer is tested.

The derivatives come from automatic differentiation. The network weights $\varphi$, $\eta$ and $G$ are trained jointly. The network output feeds a differentiable RK4 integration of the Balloon–Windkessel model, so that the BOLD and its FC can be compared with the data.

**Parametrisation.** $\eta_i$ and $G$ are parametrised as dimensionless deviations in units of the standard deviations of the prior,

$$
\eta_i = \eta_0 + a\,m_i, \qquad \eta_0 = -4.6 + \sigma_\eta\,\tilde\eta_0, \qquad a = \sigma_a\,\tilde a, \qquad G = G_0 + \sigma_G\,\tilde G \tag{15}
$$

where $m_i$ is a fixed regional map, standardised to zero mean and unit variance over the $N$ regions and declared in Section 3.1, so that $\eta_0$ is the mean excitability and $a$ its gradient along the map (Kong et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2024). The three parameters are written as dimensionless deviations in units of the standard deviations of the prior, so that a single learning rate means a comparable relative step for all of them. Free regional excitabilities, $\eta_i = -4.6 + \sigma_\eta\,\tilde\eta_i$ with one value per region, are the other candidate: H1 decides which of the two is the estimation target, and H3 compares the free $\eta_i$ with the map.

**Optimisation.** Adam with initial learning rate $10^{-3}$ and a stepwise decay, as in the PINN of Pinning the Balloon (2026; $10^{-3}$, decay factor 0.15 every 1000 iterations) and the PINN of resting-state dynamics (2024; $10^{-3}$, 40 000 iterations). A final polish with L-BFGS is optional. L-BFGS needs a fixed batch, so it runs on a single draw of the $K=4096$ collocation times of Section 5; Raissi et al. use it when the training set has hundreds to thousands of points, as here, and Rathore et al. (2024) report that Adam followed by L-BFGS outperforms either alone.

**Stopping.** The number of iterations is fixed by a pilot run and declared; it is not chosen a priori. Training stops early when both the loss and the parameters $\eta_0$, $a$ and $G$ have stabilised, and the full trajectories of $\eta_0$, $a$, $G$ and the loss are recorded. A run that reaches the iteration cap, or whose loss plateaus while the parameters are still moving, is marked as not converged and is not interpreted. The thresholds of "stable" are set with the pilot run. Stopping on the loss alone would have accepted the first run (Section 7), where the loss stabilised while the parameters had not moved.

Two-phase schedules and different learning rates for $\eta$ and $G$ are not adopted: they have no support in the references of this work, and are evaluated only if the pilot run shows that $\eta$ and $G$ do not move.

## 5. Loss function

The loss combines four terms:

$$
\mathcal L = \lambda_{\mathrm{phys}} \tilde{\mathcal L}_{\mathrm{phys}} + \lambda_{\mathrm{data}} \tilde{\mathcal L}_{\mathrm{data}} + \lambda_\theta \mathcal L_\theta + \mathcal L_u \tag{16}
$$

Each term is dimensionless, and any scale it is divided by has a meaning defined by the problem, never a value taken from the current state of training. Dividing by the running value of another loss makes the weight collapse as the network improves, which is what happened in the first run.

**Physics.** The equations are non-dimensionalised before the loss is built, which is the first step of the training pipeline of "An Expert's Guide to Training Physics-informed Neural Networks" (arXiv 2308.08468) and the scaling of Theodosiou and Rekatsinas (2025, Section 2.2). With $t^*=t/\tau_m$ and $r_i^*=\tau_m r_i$, and with $V_i$, $\eta_i$, $J$, $\Delta$ and $G$ already dimensionless, Eqs. (1) and (2) become

$$
\frac{dr_i^*}{dt^*} = \frac{\Delta}{\pi} + 2 r_i^* V_i \tag{17}
$$

$$
\frac{dV_i}{dt^*} = V_i^2 + \eta_i - (\pi r_i^*)^2 + J r_i^* + G \sum_j A_{ij} r_j^* + u_i(t) \tag{18}
$$

where $u_i$ is the forcing of Eq. (24). Without $u_i$, this is the form of Montbrió et al. (2015, Eq. 12), with $\tau_m$ as the only time scale. The change of variables was checked here symbolically and by integrating both forms for an isolated region, whose trajectories agree to the tolerance of the integrator. The residuals are the left side minus the right side of Eqs. (17) and (18) evaluated on the network output,

$$
\mathcal R_{1,i} = \frac{d\hat r_i^*}{dt^*} - \left(\frac{\Delta}{\pi} + 2\hat r_i^*\hat V_i\right), \qquad \mathcal R_{2,i} = \frac{d\hat V_i}{dt^*} - \left(\hat V_i^2 + \eta_i - (\pi \hat r_i^*)^2 + J\hat r_i^* + G\sum_j A_{ij}\hat r_j^* + u_i(t)\right) \tag{19}
$$

where the derivatives come from automatic differentiation with respect to the network input, with $d/dt^* = \tau_m\,\frac{2}{(T-1)\,\mathrm{TR}}\,d/d\tilde t$ by Eq. (14). Each residual is divided by a fixed reference scale computed before training and never updated from the network output; a scale recomputed from the output would let the network lower the loss by inflating the terms. PINODE-SR (2025) takes this scale as the empirical standard deviation of the derivative of each observed variable, and Pinning the Balloon (2026) levels the residuals to the data term with a scalar weight; here only the BOLD is observed, so $r$ and $V$ have no data to compute such a statistic from. No source was found that fixes the scale of the residuals of a neural mass model with hidden states, so the scale is derived here: each residual is divided by the sum of the absolute values of the terms of its equation, and reads as a fraction of the size of the terms, not of the rate of change, which vanishes at a fixed point. This mirrors the backward error of a linear system, which divides the residual by the magnitude of the terms (Higham 2002, Chapter 7); its use in a PINN is an extension made in this work.

For Eq. (17) the scale is exact and the same in both stable states, because its two terms balance at a fixed point. For Eq. (18), at the fixed points of an isolated region ($G=0$) with $\Delta=0.7$, $J=14.5$ and $\eta=-4.6$, the sum of the absolute values of its terms, $\mathcal R_2^{\mathrm{down}}$ and $\mathcal R_2^{\mathrm{up}}$, is about $9.3$ in the down state ($V\approx-1.95$, $r^*\approx0.057$) and about $29.3$ in the up state ($V\approx-0.11$, $r^*\approx1.01$); these values are computed here. Rabuffo et al. (2021) and Hashemi et al. (2024) do not report the fraction of time a region spends in each state, and it depends on $G$ and on the region, so the scale of region $i$ is the average of the two values weighted by the fraction $p_i$ of time that region spends in the down state at the working point, which is fixed in the simulation stage:

$$
\mathcal R_1^{\mathrm{ref}} = \frac{2\Delta}{\pi}, \qquad \mathcal R_{2,i}^{\mathrm{ref}} = p_i\,\mathcal R_2^{\mathrm{down}} + (1-p_i)\,\mathcal R_2^{\mathrm{up}}, \qquad \mathcal R_2^{\mathrm{down/up}} = \left[V^2 + |\eta| + (\pi r^*)^2 + J r^*\right]_{\mathrm{down/up}} \tag{20}
$$

In a fit restricted to the monostable range (H2) each region has a single fixed point, and $\mathcal R_{2,i}^{\mathrm{ref}}$ is the sum of the absolute values of the terms of Eq. (18) at that point.

In the dimensional form of Eq. (1) the residual and its scale both carry a factor $1/\tau_m$, so the normalised residual is the same in either form. The values $9.3$ and $29.3$ bound the sensitivity analysis. Choosing the larger instead of the smaller lowers the weight of Eq. (2) against Eq. (1) by a factor of about ten, which $\lambda_{\mathrm{phys}}$ cannot compensate because it multiplies both. Two limits are declared: the scales of an isolated region ignore the coupling term, which shifts the effective excitability, and the residual of Eq. (2) cannot reach zero because it absorbs the part of the noise that the forcing of Eq. (24) does not represent.

The physics term averages the squared normalised residuals over the regions and over $K$ collocation times $t_k$ drawn uniformly in $[0,(T-1)\,\mathrm{TR}]$ anew at every Adam iteration, as recommended by the Expert's Guide (Section 6.2), which reports that a fixed set lets the network overfit the residuals:

$$
\tilde{\mathcal L}_{\mathrm{phys}} = \frac{1}{NK}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[\left(\frac{\mathcal R_{1,i}(t_k)}{\mathcal R_1^{\mathrm{ref}}}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\mathcal R_{2,i}(t_k)}{\mathcal R_{2,i}^{\mathrm{ref}}}\right)^2\right] \tag{21}
$$

with $K=4096$ times per iteration, the batch size of the Expert's Guide, and $K=1000$ as a sensitivity test.

SEE-PINN is demonstrated only on one-dimensional problems of mechanics, and the Expert's Guide treats forward problems of partial differential equations; applying either to this coupled system of $2N$ variables with parameters to estimate is an extension made in this work.

**Forcing.** Without forcing, the MPR model has only fixed points (Montbrió et al. 2015), so a noise-free network rests at one of them in either configuration, bistable or monostable. The forcing $u_i$ of Eq. (18) represents the slow part of the noise $\xi_i$ of Eq. (4) as a truncated Fourier series over the acquisition,

$$
u_i(t) = \sum_{k=1}^{K_u}\left[u^{\mathrm{c}}_{ik}\cos\left(\frac{2\pi k t}{T\,\mathrm{TR}}\right) + u^{\mathrm{s}}_{ik}\sin\left(\frac{2\pi k t}{T\,\mathrm{TR}}\right)\right], \qquad \frac{K_u}{T\,\mathrm{TR}} \approx \frac{1}{2\,\mathrm{TR}} \tag{24}
$$

where $T\,\mathrm{TR}$ is the duration of the acquisition, so the highest frequency is the Nyquist frequency of the BOLD, and the coefficients $u^{\mathrm{c}}_{ik}$ and $u^{\mathrm{s}}_{ik}$ are trained with the network weights and the parameters. The sum starts at $k=1$, so $u_i$ has zero mean over the window: $\eta_i$ and a constant forcing enter Eq. (18) additively at the same place and would be confounded. Left free, $u_i$ would make the physics residual vanish for any trajectory and would absorb $\eta_i$, so its coefficients carry a Gaussian prior whose variance $\sigma_u^2$ is the one the noise of Eq. (4) gives them:

$$
\mathcal L_u = \sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{K_u}\frac{\left(u^{\mathrm{c}}_{ik}\right)^2 + \left(u^{\mathrm{s}}_{ik}\right)^2}{2\sigma_u^2}, \qquad \sigma_u^2 = \frac{2\sigma_\xi^2\,\delta t}{T\,\mathrm{TR}} \tag{25}
$$

where $\delta t$ is the integration step of the forward simulations, in the same unit of time as $T\,\mathrm{TR}$. A white noise of variance $\sigma_\xi^2$ per step $\delta t$ has the two-sided spectral density $\sigma_\xi^2\,\delta t$, and its Fourier coefficients over a window of length $T\,\mathrm{TR}$ have the variance $2\sigma_\xi^2\,\delta t/(T\,\mathrm{TR})$; this derivation is made here. With $\sigma_\xi$ fixed (Section 3), $\mathcal L_u$ has no free weight of its own and enters Eq. (16) beside the prior term, as a prior on the coefficients; $\lambda_{\mathrm{phys}}$ still sets how strongly the physics, and through it the forcing, is enforced. Di and Biswal (2013) model fluctuations with a Fourier basis, as cited by Friston et al. (2014), and Li et al. (2011) estimate trajectories of state noise from fMRI with a prior on their amplitude.

Only the slow part of the noise is identifiable from the BOLD, because the haemodynamics act as a low-pass filter with a time constant of several seconds (Friston et al. 2014). The statistics of the jumps between states depend on the full noise, so the PINN works with a band-limited noise, unlike the white noise of the simulator; H4 measures the effect of this difference on synthetic data. Whether $\sigma_\xi^2$ in Eq. (4) is a variance per step or an intensity per unit time changes $\sigma_u^2$ by a factor $\delta t$; this is to be verified, and the grid of $\sigma_\xi$ (Section 3) covers it. For $T\,\mathrm{TR}\approx660$ s and TR $=2.2$ s, Eq. (24) has about 150 frequencies per region.

**Data.** The data term compares the pattern of the FC, not its level:

$$
\tilde{\mathcal L}_{\mathrm{data}} = 1 - \rho_{\mathrm{FC}} \tag{22}
$$

with $\rho_{\mathrm{FC}}$ of Eq. (13); it is dimensionless and lies in $[0,2]$. The level of the simulated FC depends on the scale $\chi$, which is not fixed yet (Section 1), and on the noise, so fitting the level could let $\eta_i$ compensate a mismatch that belongs to $\chi$; the success criterion of H4 is also $\rho_{\mathrm{FC}}$.

**Domain of the forward path.** The data term needs a hemodynamic trajectory inside the domain of Eqs. (6) to (9). While the network is far from a solution its output can drive $f_i$ to zero (Section 3), and the BOLD of Eq. (10) is then undefined. An iteration whose integration of Eqs. (6) to (9) returns a non-finite state, or whose data term sends a non-finite gradient because the trajectory, though finite, presses against the edge of that domain where $f_i$ is close to zero, omits the data term and updates on the physics and prior terms alone; the physics term pulls the network toward the fixed points of Eqs. (17) and (18) (Section 7), where the input $z_i$ is small. The number of such iterations is recorded, and a run that ends with the data term omitted has not fitted the FC and is not interpreted.
**Prior.** Gaussian:

$$
\mathcal L_\theta = \frac{(\eta_0+4.6)^2}{2\sigma_\eta^2} + \frac{a^2}{2\sigma_a^2} + \frac{(G-G_0)^2}{2\sigma_G^2} \tag{23}
$$

with $\sigma_\eta=1$, $\sigma_a=0.5$, $\sigma_G=0.5\,G_0$ and $\lambda_\theta=1$; with free $\eta_i$ (Section 4) the first two terms are replaced by $\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\eta_i+4.6)^2/(2\sigma_\eta^2)$, one term per region.

With the terms normalised, each $\lambda$ is chosen in $[0,1]$, and the proposal reports a sensitivity analysis over a grid of values. Deriving the weights from noise levels, as in Bayesian PINNs (Yang et al. 2021) and in uncertainty-based loss weighting (Kendall et al. 2018), is a later refinement: the physics weight would follow from the standard deviation $\sigma_\xi$ of the noise that the residual of Eq. (2) is read to represent, and the data weight from the natural variability of the FC. That choice is a design decision of this work, not an established practice for FC.

**Alternative.** Adaptive weights, which change during training (weights from gradient norms or from the neural tangent kernel in the Expert's Guide; Gaussian-likelihood weights, among others reviewed by Theodosiou and Rekatsinas 2025), are not the main choice: they add hyperparameters and are harder to reproduce, as Theodosiou and Rekatsinas argue. They are kept as a declared alternative, to be used if a term is seen to dominate the loss during the pilot run. The gradient-norm scheme of the Expert's Guide is the first candidate.

## 6. Success criteria and interpretability

Version 1 asks that the simulated FC match the measured FC. The pointwise error between the simulated and measured BOLD is reported as a complementary metric of the network fit and is not a criterion of success: with a stochastic model, two simulations with identical parameters and different noise do not coincide pointwise, and spectral DCM (Friston et al. 2014; Novelli et al. 2024) fits second-order statistics for that reason.

The FC also varies between simulations, so $\rho_{\mathrm{FC}}$ never reaches 1. It is read against two ceilings: the correlation between the FC of simulations with the same parameters and different seeds, and the correlation between the FC of two sessions of the same subject. The seed ceiling is always used. The session ceiling is used only when the instance provides a second session of the subject; otherwise $\rho_{\mathrm{FC}}$ is read against the seed ceiling alone. Section 3.1 states whether the subject of version 1 has one.

**Statistics of H1 and H2.** The regime and the parameters are read from the FC of Eq. (12), the functional connectivity dynamics (FCD) and its variance, and scale-free regional statistics: the skewness, the kurtosis and the autocorrelation of each normalised regional series. The absolute mean and variance of the BOLD are excluded, because preprocessing (detrending and global signal regression, GSR) removes them and the scanner units are arbitrary; Ziaeemehr et al. (2025) warn that moments such as the mean and the variance "may be lost during preprocessing steps". The simulated BOLD receives the same preprocessing as the chosen real data.

**Tolerance of H1.** Each parameter is read by its posterior z-score and posterior shrinkage (Betancourt 2014; Hashemi et al. 2024) and, for the point estimators, by the spread of repeated fits across seeds and initial values. The initial thresholds, $|z|\leq 2$ and a shrinkage $\geq 0.5$, are a proposal of the authors and are revisited with the pilot runs. The tolerance for the regime is the fraction of synthetic sets whose configuration is classified correctly, with a threshold fixed by the pilot runs.

**Reliability across sessions.** It is measured with an intraclass correlation coefficient whose form is declared with the dataset; Saberi et al. (2025, cuBNM) use ICC(2,k).

The parameters are called interpretable when three conditions hold. First, H1 holds on synthetic data generated near the estimated values, for the estimation target it selected, the free $\eta_i$ or the map of Eq. (15). Second, they are stable across seeds, across the grid of $\lambda$ values and, when available, across sessions. Third, the run converged under the stopping rule of Section 4. Whether the estimated excitabilities lie in the bistable range is a result (H2), not a condition.

## 7. Limitations and open problems

- With one free excitability per region, the FC does not determine the $\eta_i$ even when the fit is good (Ziaeemehr et al. 2025; Wischnewski et al. 2025); this is why the map of Eq. (15) is a candidate target, and H1 decides between it and the free $\eta_i$. Models with regional parameters on a connectome are known to produce non-unique solutions (Xie et al. 2018).
- Implementation diagnostics, not evidence about the problem. First run: $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{data}}$ fell from 0.995 to 0.28 ($\rho_{\mathrm{FC}}\approx0.72$) while $\eta$ moved about $5\cdot10^{-5}$ and $G$ about $5\cdot10^{-6}$, so the fit was made by the network and not by the parameters. Direct stochastic simulation (Heun, step 0.1 ms, additive noise on $V$) with homogeneous $\eta=-4.6$ gave $\rho\approx0.03$–$0.045$ over a sweep of $G$; that run used $\tau_m=10$ ms and a noise level not tied to the value of this proposal, so it does not test the present configuration, and between 6.7 and 6.8 times $G_0$ all regions jump together, which global signal regression may remove; this is unconfirmed and requires plotting $r_i(t)$. Both runs were possibly ill-conditioned, since the scaling of the loss terms by the time scales of the model conditions training (Rohrhofer et al. 2023), and they are kept only to check the implementation.
- Deferred to the simulation stages: the value of $G_0$ and the useful range of $G$; the check, at $\sigma_\xi^2=0.03$ and $\tau_m=1$ ms, that regions switch between states; the convention of the noise level (variance or standard deviation) and the integrator; the scale $\chi$; heterogeneous $\eta_i$ in the generator; and the fraction of time each region spends in each state, which fixes the scale $\mathcal R_{2,i}^{\mathrm{ref}}$ of Eq. (2) (Section 5); and the size of the transient of the initial state away from rest (Section 3).
- The architecture is the reference of the cited PINNs, not tuned to this problem; the bottleneck of $W=100$ hidden units against $2N$ outputs, when $2N>W$, is a declared limit.
- The network represents the slow component of a dynamics whose time scale is $\tau_m$. Multilayer perceptrons learn low frequencies first, the spectral bias described in the Expert's Guide (Section 4.2 and Appendix A), and by Eq. (14) the derivative in Eq. (19) is $d/dt^*\approx3.0\times10^{-6}\,d/d\tilde t$ for $\tau_m=1$ ms and the instance of version 1. For a network whose derivatives with respect to $\tilde t$ are of order 1 to 100, the derivative term of Eq. (19) is negligible, and the physics loss pushes the network toward a fixed point of Eqs. (17) and (18) at every time: a sequence of slowly moving equilibria rather than the millisecond dynamics. This is an estimate made here, to be checked in the pilot run; random Fourier features (Expert's Guide, Section 4.2) are the first alternative if the pilot shows that the network does not capture the dynamics.
- Implementation details: centre the simulated BOLD before global signal regression; use an unbiased standard deviation or `torch.corrcoef` in the FC; create $\eta$ and $G$ in the same cell as the optimiser. The dataset descriptor states that its FC is a Pearson correlation and mentions no Fisher $z$ transformation.
- Future versions add delays $D_{ij}/c$ and diagnostics with a shuffled connectome and several seeds. About 85% of the time per epoch (about 20 s) is the sequential Balloon loop, which a GPU does not accelerate.
- Two failure modes belong to this method, not to the problem: a flexible network can absorb the fit while the parameters barely move (first run, above), and unbalanced residuals across hidden states can leave the dynamics underconstrained (Section 5).
- The validation of simulation-based inference of Ziaeemehr et al. (2025) was synthetic only, and its reviewers asked for real data and for comparison with other methods; H4 and H3 answer both on one subject, which is not yet a population.
- Recovering the hidden trajectories on real data is not validated here. Simultaneous EEG is the route to do it: Schirner et al. (2018) inject EEG-measured regional activity into a whole-brain model to predict BOLD, the reverse direction of this work.
- Other routes from BOLD to hidden states exist: stochastic DCM (Li et al. 2011), which Razi et al. (2015) found to perform worse than spectral DCM at rest; hemodynamic deconvolution (Karahanoğlu and Van De Ville); and convSSM (Volkmann et al. 2024), with learned dynamics and mainly synthetic data. None uses a bistable biophysical model on real BOLD, which is the setting of H2 and H4.
- Gradient-based fitting through a differentiable simulator (TVB-Optim 2025) is the closest alternative to the PINN, but it has been shown with averaged FC and without the MPR model; it is not a benchmark of H4 until it runs on this model.
- The provisional instance of Section 3.1 comes from 1000BRAINS, where Lavanga et al. (2023) fitted $G$ and the noise of the MPR model per subject in 649 subjects and found that $G$ increases with age. If that instance is kept, the estimated $G$ is compared qualitatively with their range for the subject's age; the models differ (delays, noise, normalisation of $A$), so the comparison is a plausibility check, not a test. Age effects on regional excitation-inhibition balance (Zhang et al. 2024) and regional inhibition in dementia (2024) motivate the regional parametrisation for later population studies, not this single-subject version.
- Reliability across sessions is modest even with dedicated estimators: simulated FC reached an ICC of 0.41 to 0.46 and parameters poor to fair reliability (Saberi et al. 2025; Domhof et al. 2022). H3 across sessions is read against these values.
- The wording of the reliability of cuBNM differs by version: the bioRxiv v1 reports "poor-to-good" and the eLife Reviewed Preprint "poor-to-fair"; the eLife version is cited.
- The use of regional parameters of the MPR model as subject-level markers (EF2, Section 1) is not yet shown in the reviewed sources; this is to be verified. No reviewed source reports the test-retest reliability of the parameters of the MPR model; a bounded search for it is owed.
- DC3 (Section 1) has only partial support in the abstracts of its sources; the direct evidence that the regime is fixed before fitting is in their body texts.
- Recovery of regional states is limited to slow states: only states that last long enough for the BOLD to resolve them, of the order of seconds, are recoverable, and the PINN works with a band-limited noise (Section 5).
- The bistable and the monostable configuration are defined on the decoupled node, by the convention of the literature (Section 3): the range $(-5.35,-2.45)$ holds for an isolated region, the coupling shifts the effective excitability, and restricting $\eta_0$ does not restrict $\eta_i=\eta_0+a\,m_i$, so the label of the node does not guarantee the label of the network, which is judged by the variance of the FCD.
- The dataset is not fixed. The instance of Section 3.1 has $T=300$ volumes, and averaging over space and time, observation noise and limited samples can mask nonlinear dynamics (Nozari et al. 2024; Nature Biomedical Engineering 8, online December 2023, cited as 2024 throughout). H1 states the number of volumes the comparison of regimes needs, and a dataset with longer acquisitions, or with simultaneous EEG to validate the reconstructed states, is chosen accordingly.
- The search for an earlier comparison of a bistable and a monostable configuration of one biophysical model on real resting-state fMRI (Section 1) was bounded: three queries on 2026-11-09. Its absence is stated to the best of our knowledge.

# Idea central (pre-proposal)

Neural mass models of the MPR type (Montbrio-Pazo-Roxin), coupled through the structural
connectome, can reproduce whole-brain fMRI signals. Hypothesis: parameters can be found
that generate the exact population dynamics on the connectome and fit fMRI data.

Sketches: DiagramaPT1.png (architecture), DiagramaPT2.png (loss), DiagramaPT3.png (notation).

## Pipeline (from the sketches)
- PINN: input t -> outputs r_i(t) (rate) and v_i(t) (voltage) for N regions; weights w.
- Autodiff gives dr/dt, dv/dt, substituted into the MPR equations:
  - tau_m r_i' = Delta/(pi tau_m) + 2 r_i v_i
  - tau_m v_i' = v_i^2 + eta_i - (pi tau_m r_i)^2 + tau_m J r_i + tau_m G sum_j C_ij r_j(t - D_ij/v)
  - C_ij = A_ij (.) W_ij, or C_ij = A_ij
- Hemodynamic integrator (balloon): s' = r - kappa s - gamma(f-1); f' = s; tau_h v' = f - v^(1/alpha);
  tau_h q' = f E(f)/E0 - v^(1/alpha) q/v; E(f) = 1-(1-E0)^(1/f). Rest: s=0, f=v=q=1.
- BOLD: y_i = V0[k1(1-q) + k2(1-q/v) + k3(1-v)], k1=4.3 nu0 E0 TE, k2=eps r0 E0 TE, k3=1-eps.
- Y_hat (N x K) -> FC_sim = Corr(rows i, j), N x N.

## Parameters
- theta_F = [eta_i, Delta, J, tau_m]; theta_G = [kappa, gamma, alpha, E0, V0, eps]; theta = [theta_F, theta_G].

## Loss
- Residuals R_r, R_v = LHS - RHS of the MPR equations, at collocation times t_1..t_K.
- L_fisica = 1/(NK) sum_k sum_i [lambda_r R_r^2 + lambda_v R_v^2]
- L_theta = sum_p (theta_p - mu_p)^2 / (2 sigma_p^2) (Gaussian prior; log-normal if positive)
- L_datos = 1 - rho, rho = Corr(a, b), a/b = upper triangle (i<j) of FC_sim / FC_real
  (diagonal is trivial; lower triangle repeats the upper). Compares pattern, not level.
- L = lambda_fisica L_fisica + lambda_datos L_datos + lambda_theta L_theta
- Joint gradient descent on w and theta; repeat.

## Notation (DiagramaPT3)
N regions i=1..N; collocation times K={t_1..t_K}; A connectome; D fibre lengths; tau_ij = D_ij / v.

# Hybrid PINN for neural-mass parameter estimation (v1) — summary from the implementation session

Pasted from the other session; kept in Spanish as received.

## 1. Objetivo
Estimar parámetros de un modelo de cerebro completo para que el BOLD simulado reproduzca la FC en reposo de un sujeto real.
Piezas: conectoma estructural (dMRI), masa neuronal MPR por región, modelo hemodinámico. Problema inverso resuelto con una PINN (PyTorch).

Hipótesis:
- H1: la FC simulada se parece a la FC real más de lo que se parece el conectoma estructural solo.
- H2: con datos sintéticos de parámetros conocidos, el método los recupera.
- H3: simular la MPR directamente con los parámetros estimados, sin la red, reproduce la FC real.

## 2. Datos
- 1000BRAINS v1.1, atlas Desikan-Killiany, N = 70, un sujeto.
- S: conectividad estructural N×N; D: longitudes de fibra en mm (no se usa en v1); FC: Pearson empírica; bold: 300 volúmenes.
- TR = 2.2 s, TE = 30 ms, 3 T.
- Preproceso del dataset: regresión de molestias con GSR, pasa-altas, detrend lineal, sin pasa-banda. Al BOLD simulado también se le aplica GSR.
- Conectoma usado: A = S con diagonal 0 (la autoexcitación ya está en J), normalizado a máximo 1.

## 3. MPR sin retardos (t en s, tau_m = 10 ms)
- tau_m dr_i/dt = Delta/(pi tau_m) + 2 r_i V_i
- tau_m dV_i/dt = V_i^2 + eta_i - (pi tau_m r_i)^2 + tau_m J r_i + tau_m G sum_j A_ij r_j

| Parámetro | v1 |
| --- | --- |
| Delta | 0.7 fijo (Rabuffo) |
| J | 14.5 fijo (Rabuffo) |
| tau_m | 0.01 s fijo |
| eta_i | uno por región, se estima; inicio -4.6 (rango biestable -5.35..-2.45) |
| G | acople global, se estima |
| Retardos | ninguno en v1; luego D_ij/c, c = 4 m/s |

- G0 = 1/(suma media por fila de A).
- Equilibrios de región aislada con eta = -4.6: bajo ≈ 5.7 Hz, frontera inestable ≈ 45 Hz, alto ≈ 101 Hz.

## 4. Hemodinámica
- Balloon-Windkessel (Friston 2000): s, f, nu, q; kappa 0.65, gamma 0.41, tau_h 0.98, alpha 0.32, E0 0.34.
- Entrada: z_i = chi (r_i - rbar_i), con rbar_i = promedio en el tiempo de la región i. Con r crudo el reposo se corre: f* = 1 + rbar/gamma.
- chi fija, 0.1 en entrenamiento (std de z ~ 0.1; si z es grande, f < 0 y explota).
- BOLD: Stephan 2007 ec. 12, V0 = 4, eps = 0.47, vartheta0 = 84.8, varrho0 = 110, TE = 0.03.
- RK4 a mano en PyTorch, diferenciable, sin in-place; dt_h = 0.2 s; reposo inicial s=0, f=nu=q=1; BOLD cada TR. Validado contra scipy.

## 5. PINN
- Una red para todas las regiones: t -> 2N = 140 salidas (r_1..r_N, V_1..V_N).
- 2 capas ocultas de 512, tanh; t escalado a [-1, 1].
- r pasa por softplus; V libre. Derivadas por autodiff respecto a t.
- Entrenables: pesos (varphi), eta (N), G; Adam lr 1e-3; G recortado a >= 0.

## 6. Pérdida
L = lambda_fis L_fis + lambda_datos L_datos + lambda_theta L_theta

- L_fis: K = 1000 instantes aleatorios por época; R_r = tau_m dr/dt - (Delta/(pi tau_m) + 2 r V); R_V = tau_m dV/dt - (V^2 + eta - (pi tau_m r)^2 + tau_m J r + tau_m G sum_j A_ij r_j);
  L_fis = 1/(NK) sum_k sum_i [lambda_rv R_r^2 + (1 - lambda_rv) R_V^2], lambda_rv = 0.5.
  R_r está en Hz y pesa más que R_V; balance pendiente. R_V no debería llegar a 0 (absorbe el ruido).
- L_datos: red en malla regular -> z -> Balloon (RK4) -> BOLD en TR -> GSR -> FC sim (Pearson); comparada con FC real en 2415 pares del triángulo superior:
  L_datos = MSE(FC_sim, FC_real) + (1 - rho_FC). No se compara el BOLD punto a punto (actividad espontánea, unidades distintas).
- L_theta: eta_i ~ N(-4.6, 1^2); G ~ N(G0, (0.5 G0)^2); lambda_theta = 1.
- lambda_datos = 1; lambda_fis = L_datos/L_fis recalculado cada 10 épocas (medido solo en época 0, la física dejó de pesar). Alternativa con fundamento pendiente: lambda_fis = N T / (2 sigma^2).
- Parada: hasta 1000 épocas; cada 50 se revisa eta (< 0.01), G (< 1 %), mejora de L_datos (< 0.005); se detiene ante nan.

## 7. Resultados
- Primera corrida (100 épocas, lambda_fis fijado en época 0): L_datos 0.995 -> 0.28 (rho_FC ≈ 0.72); L_fis 258 -> 0.41; peso de la física ~1 -> ~0.0016.
- eta cambió ~5e-5 y G ~5e-6: el ajuste lo hizo la red, no los parámetros. La red compensa cualquier eta ajustando V, así que el gradiente de eta no tiene dirección consistente. Para que los parámetros se muevan, la física debe pesar en serio.
- Cambios posteriores: lambda_fis recalculado cada 10 épocas; término MSE en L_datos.

## 8. Validación directa (H3)
- MPR sin la red, con eta y G aprendidos, ruido aditivo en V (sigma = 0.5, provisional), paso 0.5 ms; mismo Balloon, BOLD, GSR, FC; chi recalculado para la r simulada.
- Resultado: FC plana, rho ≈ 0.
- Barrido de G (eta = -4.6, sigma = 0.5): rho ≈ 0 de 0 a 6 G0 (0.033 en 6 G0, 0.035 en 6.5 G0); FC media = -0.014 ≈ -1/(N-1) (lo que da la GSR con señales independientes); fracción en estado alto = 0 (todo en ~5.7 Hz temblando por ruido); desde 7 G0 explota.
- Diagnóstico: la FC en reposo de la MPR aparece cuando las regiones saltan entre estado bajo y alto y los saltos se propagan por el conectoma (cascadas, Rabuffo). Con estos sigma y G no hay saltos. La explosión en 7 G0 es numérica: hubs en estado alto con oscilaciones rápidas que Euler no resuelve (ni con 0.05 ms). Euler a 0.5 ms sacaba artificialmente regiones del estado alto.
- Solo en la validación se cambió a Heun estocástico, paso 0.1 ms (estable). El entrenamiento no se tocó: la MPR solo aparece en los residuos.
- Pendiente: barrido con Heun en 6.5-10 G0 buscando saltos y rho > 0.

## 9. Pendientes
- Calibrar sigma y G0 con el barrido directo; luego lambda_fis = N T / (2 sigma^2); obtener chi desde la simulación directa.
- Entrenar en dos fases (solo L_fis, luego L_datos); lr propio mayor (1e-2) para eta y G; considerar L-BFGS en la fase de física.
- H2 con datos sintéticos (identificabilidad); conectoma barajado, varias semillas, sensibilidad al uso de la desviación como entrada del Balloon; retardos (D, c = 4 m/s) después.
- Implementación: crear eta y G en la misma celda que el optimizador; si la malla empieza en t = -20 s, desplazar índices del TR; confirmar que la FC del archivo está en [-1, 1] sin Fisher-z.
- Rendimiento: ~85 % del tiempo es el bucle secuencial del Balloon; dt_h = 0.2 s ayudó; otra opción: calcular L_datos cada pocas épocas.
